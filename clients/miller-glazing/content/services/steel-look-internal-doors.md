## Where they work best

The point of a Crittall-style, steel-look door is light. A solid door between a hallway and
a kitchen keeps the daylight in one room; a glazed one lets it run through
the house while still closing on noise and cooking smells. That is why most
of the ones we fit sit between a hall and a kitchen or living room, or
between a kitchen and a snug that the owners want connected but not open.

They suit older houses as well as new ones. The slim black grid was
originally an industrial window from the 1920s and 30s, which is exactly
when a lot of the housing around Dagenham, Ilford and Romford was built —
so it belongs in a 1930s semi as naturally as it does in a new-build flat.

## Single doors, pairs and screens

- **Single door** — the everyday one. Hall to kitchen, study, utility.
- **French pair** — for wider openings where you want to throw both leaves
  open. See our [internal bifold and sliding doors](/services/internal-bifold-and-sliding-doors/)
  page for openings wider than a pair can sensibly cover.
- **Fixed screen** — glass and frame with no opening leaf, often next to a
  door, to glaze a whole wall rather than a doorway.
- **Cabinets and small screens** — the same frame section works for a drinks
  cabinet or a pantry front, as one of the jobs in our gallery shows.

## Glass options

Toughened as standard. Clear for maximum light; grey or bronze tint where
you want the room beyond softened; reeded or satin where you want light
without a view — a downstairs toilet, a utility, a home office on a video
call.

## How we price it

Per opening, after measuring, as one fixed figure that includes the frame,
glass, handles, fitting and making good. Width, height, number of leaves,
glass type and handle choice all change the number, so we do not publish a
single price that would be wrong for most jobs. Send a photo and rough sizes
and we will give you a sensible range on the phone before anyone comes out.
